# A question framework is a scaffold, not a verdict

Use the smallest useful framework for a formulation or search-planning task. The **Thinking Critically with AI** pack presents the five options below (PDF pp. 22–31); [source details](source-map.md) distinguish that teaching guide from empirical evidence. These familiar frameworks were not invented by this skill or the pack. Their use does not establish a study's quality or a search's completeness.

| Question target | Useful starting framework | Components | Main limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intervention effects | PICO; add T/S when needed | Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome; Time / Study design | Defining a comparator does not supply randomization or control confounding |
| Experiences, meanings or mixed-methods views | SPIDER | Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type | Keep open inquiry open; do not pre-fill themes or force a treatment comparator |
| Naturally occurring exposure and outcome | PEO | Population, Exposure, Outcome | An exposure–outcome association does not identify causation |
| Service or policy information needs | ECLIPSE | Expectation, Client group, Location, Impact, Professionals, Service | Specify the service decision; the framework is not an evaluation design |
| How common a condition/event is | CoCoPop | Condition, Context, Population | Define case criteria and denominator; distinguish prevalence from incidence and add time/person-time when needed |

These are options, not an exhaustive taxonomy. A simple exploratory question or an established field-specific framework may fit better. If a question has distinct phases, state which phase the chosen framework supports instead of stacking all five. Missing context stays a labeled choice/unknown, not a fabricated population or outcome.

## A focused demonstration

**Initial wording, fictional:** “Why does fermented soy drink improve graduate students' fatigue?”

The wording assumes a benefit and a mechanism. Before there is verified evidence, separate the aims:

- An intervention question could use PICO: “Among [specified students], what effect, if any, does [defined drink regimen], compared with [specified comparator], have on [defined fatigue measure] over [time]?” Brackets are A-proposals to resolve, not supplied facts.
- A question about daily fatigue experiences and food choice could use SPIDER to identify sample and phenomenon, leaving themes open. It does not require a randomized trial.
- If ordinary dietary exposure is the target, PEO may organize an association question; changing to that question changes the inferential target and must be explicit.

**Already sound:** “In the supplied randomized comparison, what is the effect of A versus B on the prespecified fatigue outcome at 12 weeks?” Retain it if that matches the intended decision. The word *effect* does not assume a positive answer. Inspect the design before interpreting any causal estimate.

## From formulation to discovery

Write the question with its important components. Then build a discovery query that names the constructs clearly, expands ambiguous abbreviations, and permits conflicting findings. Try an appropriately scoped contrary/null query as a check. Save actual terms and filters if reproducibility matters. Search syntax differs by tool, and restrictive outcome/design terms can miss relevant studies; do not assume the full framework should be pasted into every database search.

On return, check whether the tool dropped a comparator, time, population or outcome. An adjacent study is an adjacent study; annotate the mismatch rather than silently answering a broader question. A framing exercise may finish with a focused question and an evidence request without running a search.
