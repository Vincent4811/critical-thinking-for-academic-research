# Design a critical-thinking lesson

Use **design-lesson** for teacher preparation, not a live learner interview. It turns the course's five habits into observable student work. A full set of teacher prompts can be prepared now; actual learner reasoning and revision records remain unfilled.

## Resolve the teaching brief

Use the stated learning goal, duration, audience, class size, language and source material. If a routine preference is absent, state an adaptable default and continue: graduate research learners, 120 minutes, triads with pairs for remainders, Traditional Chinese teacher guidance with English discussion sentence stems. Do not assume a particular number of students or that the learners are from one discipline.

An explicit duration or format overrides the default. Produce a workable plan even if no real paper is supplied, using a clearly fictional case or the attributed historical appraisal examples. Ask only if a missing fact prevents an appropriate design; otherwise complete the supported plan and flag what the instructor must select.

## Design backward from visible learning

Choose a small number of observable objectives, such as separating observation from claim, defining an outcome, tracing a source and denominator, proposing a discriminator, or justifying a revision/retention. Connect each objective to an individual artifact and an assessment cue. "Students enjoy a game" or "feel more confident" does not by itself evidence critical thinking.

Each substantive activity must include:

1. Learning target and its place in Challenge / Define / Evidence / Alternatives / Revise.
2. Time, grouping/roles, materials, rules and an example sufficient for the teacher to run it.
3. An individual initial choice or judgment **and reason before the reveal**.
4. An observation/source or rule change that can challenge that reason.
5. At least one evidence-based peer question and a transfer to a research question.
6. A learner-owned revision or justified retention, with next evidence to seek.

Debrief the assumption and evidence, not the player's intelligence, morality or willingness to agree. Game scores and winning are not critical-thinking grades. Use symbolic points only unless the teacher separately specifies a suitable alternative.

## Assemble the session

Use [the bilingual 120-minute lesson](../teaching/lesson-120.en.md) as a working example; it also gives a 50-minute adaptation. Operational game rules live once in [activity cards](../teaching/activity-cards.en.md) and their Chinese translation. Select activities to meet the objectives and budget rather than running every game.

Add times arithmetically. Include instructions, scoring/reveal, debrief, individual mapping, peer challenge, revision and an exit ticket; do not hide these costs between agenda rows. When too many requested activities cannot fit meaningfully, say so and provide a feasible prioritized rotation or a clearly labeled longer alternative. Do not silently exceed the budget.

Plan roles so everyone contributes. For triads, rotate author/questioner/evidence recorder; for pairs, split the same block evenly. With a remainder of one, use a pair or regroup rather than leave a learner without feedback. For large classes use simultaneous small groups and selected plenary examples, not every learner presenting in a fixed short slot.

## Evidence and debrief

Give enough information to support the intended inference. Separate simulated data, historical reports and verified publication status. Historical retracted findings are appraisal material, not evidence for advice; any staged revelation is explicitly a teaching simulation and always includes the status in the closing debrief.

Preserve conceptual alternatives: a classroom game's equilibrium assumptions are not proof about real people, and a cooperation choice is not a moral or personality diagnosis. The Prisoner's Dilemma protocol in the cards is a **new extension**; only its conceptual connection appeared in the supplied course summary. Never imply the original slides included the new payoff matrix.

## When the lesson involves AI research tools

Use [the AI judgment lab](../teaching/ai-judgment-lab.en.md) for prompt comparison, passage-level checking, interpretation of a body of evidence, explanation with AI set aside, and an actual decision log. Its offline packets are explicitly fictional. An explanation may be spoken, written, drawn or appropriately supported; assess source alignment and limits, not fluency. The 15-minute option replaces the existing lab and preserves the total; do not add it on top of an already full schedule.

## Assess and close

Compare the learner's initial and final explanations using [the existing four-dimension rubric](../evals/rubric.md), with a reason for each judgment. Retention can be good reasoning; a changed answer alone is not improvement. Do not claim a validated ability score or learning gain from these worksheets.

End with the revised/retained research question, E/A/U map, feedback decision, reason and one next check. Use [the bilingual lesson template](../templates/lesson-plan.en.md) and [learner worksheet](../templates/classroom-worksheet.en.md) for reusable outputs. Protect identifiable student work; public demonstrations are fictional or explicitly authorized.

**Done:** the teacher can run the session from the plan, every objective has visible learner evidence, the timeline fits the budget, and the exit task makes the reasoning change inspectable. No publishing, student-data upload, new service or experiment is authorized merely by designing the lesson.
